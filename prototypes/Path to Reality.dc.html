BuffPath · Working plan

Taking this from prototype to something students can actually use

The prototype answers what the app should be. This document covers what stands between it and a working product: whose permission you need, what to prove before writing production code, and what the app looks like if the institutional pieces never arrive.

1 · The three real constraints

Engineering is not the bottleneck. Three things gate this product, in descending order of difficulty.

Permission, not technology

Everything distinctive in the design depends on data you do not own. Handshake postings, application status sync, applicant volume, CU hiring history, and the Leeds alumni pool all sit behind CU Career Services and Leeds Alumni Relations. None is available to a student project by default, and scraping Handshake is a terms-of-service problem rather than a technical one.

Supply before demand

An internship app with thin listings is worthless, and a student who opens it to an empty board does not come back. Public boards can be aggregated through their own feeds and APIs, so that is the floor. Tier sorting still works there; applicant volume mostly does not, which is why the prototype flags that assumption on its face.

Seasonality

Internship recruiting runs in windows. You get roughly two real shots a year at students who care, and missing one costs six months. This shapes the timeline more than the build does.

2 · Conversations to have first

Each of these is a short meeting that changes what you build. Have them before writing production code.

Who What you are actually asking What their answer decides
CU Career Services Whether they would sponsor a Handshake integration, and what they would want in return Whether the app you designed is buildable, or whether it becomes the public-boards version
Leeds Alumni Relations Whether Mentor Match can be surfaced in a student app, and who owns the matching Whether the alumni tab is a real feature or a link out
A Leeds faculty advisor Which campus venture programs are currently running and taking applications Funding, credit, and whether this becomes coursework
Two or three recruiters who hire CU interns Whether a written line above a resume would change how they read an applicant Whether the Priority tier's premise holds from the employer side

3 · What to validate, in order

The cheapest useful next step is not code. It is fifteen students, the prototype on a phone, and watching where they hesitate.

1
Does anyone write the Priority line?

This is the one question no amount of design resolves. If the line reads as homework and students try to skip it, the whole differentiator collapses and you are building a nicer job board. Test this before anything else.

2
Does the three-state requirement list change behavior?

Watch whether "you worded it differently" actually sends someone to their resume, or whether it reads as noise. The met/reworded/missing split is only worth its complexity if the middle state moves people.

3
Can extraction be trusted on real postings?

Run twenty real postings through by hand and score the output yourself. If the parser is wrong often enough that students dismiss lines routinely, the feature teaches distrust rather than clarity.

4
Do first-years and juniors need the same app?

The prototype assumes yes, with class year changing content rather than structure. Worth confirming, because a first-year with an empty resume sees mostly missing requirements, and that could read as discouraging rather than honest.

4 · The fallback, if no integration happens

Assume Career Services says no, or says not yet. What survives:

Survives
Requirement extraction, the three-state match against a resume, comment-to-apply, the tracker, deadline calendar, and Last Call. All of these run on public postings and the student's own resume.
Weakens
Tiering. Without applicant counts, Priority has to be inferred from weaker signals: company size, whether the posting is a named program, how long it stays open. Workable, less defensible.
Goes away
Automatic status sync, CU hiring history, and Leeds Mentor Match as an in-app flow. Status falls back to self-reported, which the design already handles; the alumni tab becomes a link to the existing program.

Worth noting that the design already assumes this outcome in places. Every tracker row states whether its status is synced or self-reported, and the nudge card exists precisely because sync cannot be relied on. That is not an accident of the prototype; it is what makes the fallback survivable.

5 · Open questions

  • Is this a CU Boulder product or a product that starts at CU Boulder? The answer changes whether institutional dependency is a moat or a ceiling.
  • Who maintains it after you graduate? Campus tools die on this question more than any other.
  • Does the employer side ever become real software, or does it stay a claim about what recruiters see? The prototype shows a recruiter card, and that promise should be verified before it ships.
  • What does success look like at the end of one recruiting season — installs, applications submitted, or internships accepted? Only the last one matters, and it is the hardest to measure.
